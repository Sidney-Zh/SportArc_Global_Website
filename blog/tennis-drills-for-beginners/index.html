Tennis Drills for Beginners: Build Your First Comfortable Rally

These tennis drills for beginners start with one manageable bounce, then a short cooperative rally. Move back only while both players can stay in control.

The first ball goes over. The next one flies past your partner, and you both spend more time collecting balls than hitting them. That is a familiar start to tennis. It does not mean you need a harder swing or a more expensive racket. You may simply be asking too much of the first rally.

These tennis drills for beginners make that rally easier to start. First, get comfortable controlling a ball on your own. Then work with a partner at short range, where neither of you needs to hit hard. Move farther apart only when you can still send each other a playable ball. The aim is to create enough good attempts to learn from, rather than restart after every shot.

This session is for adults who are new to tennis or returning after a long break. You need a racket that feels manageable, a few balls and a clear playing area. A partner is useful for the later drills. Lower-compression balls can make learning easier for adults too; you do not have to begin with the fastest ball available. If you are at a club, ask which practice balls and space you can use.

Make the first rally manageable

Begin closer to the net than the baseline. The service boxes give you an obvious area for a short, cooperative exchange. Both players should have room to swing and move without stepping on loose balls. Pick those up before starting, and keep spare balls away from your feet.

Agree on what counts as success. For this session, a good shot gives your partner a comfortable chance to send the ball back. You are not trying to surprise them, find a sideline or finish the point. Aim into a generous space with enough height over the net. A beautiful winner is not especially helpful when the task is to keep a rally alive.

Use a compact, unhurried swing. Turn your shoulders as you prepare, make small adjustments with your feet and leave space between the ball and your body. You should not need to reach at the last moment or pull your elbow into your ribs to find the ball. If the feed is awkward, stop and start again instead of practising a desperate rescue.

Grip advice can get complicated quickly. For a basic forehand, an eastern or a moderate semi-western grip can be a workable starting point. You do not need to learn every grip in one session. If you have a coach, ask them to check the grip you are using and how the racket face meets the ball. Avoid squeezing the handle as hard as you can or deliberately locking the wrist in place.

Drill 1: Control one bounce before adding a partner

Find a clear space where you can make a small racket movement without hitting anyone. Let the ball bounce, then tap it gently upwards with the racket. Let it bounce again. Keep the height manageable and move your feet to stay comfortably beside the ball rather than leaning farther and farther after it.

At first, you can catch the ball after each tap and reset. That gives you time to notice where it went. If every tap sends it forwards, look at the racket face and the direction of the small swing. If it shoots above your reach, reduce the force. You are learning how a small change at contact affects a ball you can see and control.

  1. Drop the ball into a comfortable space beside and slightly in front of you.
  2. Let it bounce, then make a gentle upward tap.
  3. Watch the height and direction; catch and reset if needed.
  4. When that is comfortable, keep going through another bounce without catching.

This is a coordination exercise, not the exact swing you will use from the baseline. Keep it simple. A lower-bouncing ball may make the rhythm easier to find. If the exercise turns into chasing the ball around the court, return to one tap and a catch rather than trying to save the sequence.

Once you can control the bounce, try a gentle drop-feed forehand towards a large, safe target area. Turn before swinging, give the ball space and send it forwards without trying to hit through the back fence. Notice whether you can finish balanced. The aim is to carry the same sense of control into a forward shot.

Drill 2: A short rally with a helpful partner

Stand opposite each other near the service lines, adjusting the distance to your level. Start with a gentle underarm feed. Let the ball bounce, return it towards your partner and prepare again. Keep the first exchange slow enough that both players have time to turn and move.

The feed matters. If the opening ball lands at someone's feet or races past their shoulder, the rally has started with a problem the exercise was meant to remove. A partner who feeds patiently is helping more than one who can hit spectacular shots. Reset an unplayable feed without treating it as the receiver's failure.

Begin by sending the ball to a comfortable forehand area. That lets you work on spacing and contact without changing strokes every time. You can introduce the backhand side when both players are ready, but keep the same cooperative aim. This article is not asking you to master two strokes and a serve before you enjoy a rally.

After each hit, let the racket come back in front and look for the next ball. Avoid standing still to admire a good shot. Your feet may need a small adjustment even when your partner is trying to hit straight back. Make that adjustment early enough that the swing does not have to solve everything at once.

If rallies keep ending immediately, shorten the distance or use a slower ball. You can also alternate a feed and a single return, then catch and reset. That is still useful tennis practice. Once the single return is comfortable, try linking the next shot rather than making the feed faster.

Drill 3: Add distance while keeping the same control

When the short exchange feels repeatable, move a little farther apart. Do not jump straight from the service boxes to a full-speed baseline rally. At the new distance, check whether you can still send a ball with a useful height and enough time for your partner to respond.

You will need the ball to travel farther, but that does not mean swinging as hard as possible. Let the forward swing grow gradually while keeping your contact comfortable. If you start reaching, rushing or spraying the ball, the new distance is asking too much for now. Move closer again and regain the exchange.

Keep the target generous. Trying to add distance and hit a narrow corner at the same time makes it hard to understand why a shot failed. Change one thing first. Once you can rally from the new position, you can agree on a broad direction or alternate between two easy locations.

Notice what breaks down. Is the ball landing short because you are barely touching it? Is it going long because you suddenly speed up the swing? Or is the contact awkward because you stopped adjusting your feet? Those are different problems. A shorter rally that reveals one of them is more useful than a long session spent guessing.

These tennis drills for beginners do not have a pass mark you must reach before moving back. Use the quality of the exchange. If both players can prepare, contact the ball comfortably and recover, test a little more distance. If one player is scrambling on most shots, make the task easier for both of you.

Common problems during beginner tennis drills

The ball keeps arriving too close to your body. Watch where you stand after the bounce. You may be waiting for the ball to enter your swing space rather than moving to create that space. Return to a predictable feed and take small positioning steps before the forward swing.

Every attempt goes long. Reduce the pace and look at the racket face at contact. Avoid trying to fix all long balls by chopping sharply downwards. Give yourself a simple feed and a broad target so you can see what a calmer swing does. A coach can help if the same contact problem keeps returning.

You hit into the net when you try to be careful. Being controlled does not mean steering the racket through a tiny, hesitant movement. Give the ball some height and let the swing continue naturally. Start closer so you can find that comfortable shape without also worrying about distance.

One partner is much stronger than the other. Make the exercise fit the newer player. The more experienced partner can feed gently, aim into an easy area and wait for a balanced reset. Turning every short ball into an attack will not help you build a cooperative rally.

You improve during a drill and lose it when playing points. Keep the first point game simple. Start with the same easy feed and a broad playing area. Adding scoring, an unfamiliar serve and difficult placement all at once changes the task considerably. Bring those demands in gradually rather than treating a messy first game as proof that practice did not work.

Leave the session with something you can repeat

Choose one of these tennis drills for beginners as your main task next time. You might need more time controlling the bounce, or you may be ready for a short partner rally. Start from the version where you can recognise a good contact and understand an obvious miss.

If you record a few exchanges, place the camera safely outside the playing area and include both players where possible. Watch whether the feed was playable, where you met the ball and whether you were ready afterwards. Look for one recurring problem instead of comparing every detail with a professional swing.

Before finishing, return to the easiest useful version and notice what changed. Perhaps the ball now gives your partner more time, or you are making space without thinking about every step. Write down that observation. At the next session, begin there and build the rally again. You can learn the rest of the game without trying to learn it all today.
