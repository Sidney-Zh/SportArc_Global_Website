Badminton Footwork Drills: Reach the Shuttle and Recover for the Next Shot

Build your badminton footwork drills around a controlled lunge, then add a starting cue and a choice of direction. Include the recovery each time.

You reach the drop shot, get your racket under the shuttle and send it back. Then you are stuck at the front of the court while the next shot sails behind you. Getting there was only half the job. The movement after contact needs practice too.

These badminton footwork drills work on that whole sequence: prepare, move, play and recover. Start with a short, predictable movement towards the net. Once you can stop comfortably and push back, add the timing of the first step. Only then ask a partner to make the direction less predictable. You do not need to sprint to all six corners to get something useful from this session.

Use a clear, dry court and shoes suited to indoor court movement. Pick up loose shuttles before starting. You need a racket; a partner and a few shuttles help with the later exercises. The examples describe a right-handed player. If you are left-handed, reverse the racket-side references. Warm up with easy movement first, and keep the reach short enough that you can control the landing.

What these badminton footwork drills should improve

Watch what happens just before you move. Are your feet planted while you wait to see where the shuttle goes? Or are you bouncing so high that you are still coming down when you need to push sideways? Both can leave you feeling late, even if you can run quickly.

A small split step is a brief preparation to move, timed around the opponent's hit. It is not a high jump. Keep the movement modest and your legs ready to push in the direction you see. When practising with a partner, watch their action rather than bouncing to your own private rhythm. If you cannot connect the movement to a real cue yet, slow the exercise down.

You also need a finish you can leave. A huge lunge might let you touch a difficult shuttle, but it is a poor starting point for beginner practice if you cannot get back out of it. Work within a range where you can reach, balance and recover without an extra scramble. As that becomes easier, you can extend the distance.

Recovery does not mean racing to the exact geometric centre after every shot. Your useful position depends on the shot you played and what can come back. For these drills, choose a convenient starting area that leaves room to repeat the movement. In a rally, be ready to adjust that area rather than treating one mark on the floor as home at all costs.

Drill 1: A short lunge you can recover from

Begin a comfortable distance from the front service line, facing the net. Choose one front-court direction and keep it the same for now. Without a shuttle, move towards that space and finish with your racket-side leg forward. Reach the racket out as if taking a low shuttle, then push back into a balanced ready position.

Take your time on the first few attempts. The landing should feel controlled, with the foot pointing broadly into the direction of the lunge rather than twisting underneath you. Let the front knee bend as you accept your weight. Keep the torso supported instead of folding at the waist and reaching with the head. Your free arm can help you balance; it does not need to be frozen in a particular pose.

  1. Start ready, with the racket in front and enough space around you.
  2. Move towards the same front-court point and make a comfortable racket-side lunge.
  3. Pause long enough to notice whether you are balanced, then push away from the front leg and recover.
  4. Reset before repeating. Do not rush the next attempt to rescue a poor landing.

The pause is a learning aid, not a demand to stop dead in a match. It makes an unstable finish easier to notice. If you wobble, take a shorter step or begin closer to the target. If your foot slides or the movement hurts, stop and check the setup instead of trying to make the repetition count.

Once the movement feels controlled, remove the deliberate pause. Let the reach lead into the push back. Then add a gentle, predictable shuttle feed to the same place. Keep the shot simple: you are checking whether contacting the shuttle changes the movement. If the racket task makes you lose your balance, return to the version without a shuttle for a while.

Drill 2: Add the start, without adding a race

Use the same direction and a similar distance. This time, ask your partner to give an obvious visual cue before you move. They can begin with a clear racket movement or a slow, predictable feed. Make a small preparatory split step around that action, then push towards the known target.

The purpose is to connect your start to something outside your own rhythm. You already know where you are going, so you can pay attention to when you begin. Avoid turning it into a contest to leave the ground first. A split step that is early enough for you to settle completely and wait again has not helped much; one that keeps you airborne while the shuttle travels is also getting in your way.

Keep the feed easy enough that you have time to recognise the cue, move and recover. Your partner should wait until you are ready before starting another repetition. Rapid-fire feeding can hide the problem by forcing you to chase continuously. At this stage, a clean start and a controlled return are more useful than looking busy.

If you keep mistiming it, remove the shuttle and agree on one clear signal. Practise responding to that signal with a small preparation and a single push. Then restore the short movement. If it is comfortable, change the pause between cues slightly so you have to watch rather than count seconds in your head. Keep the direction predictable until the timing begins to make sense.

Drill 3: Let your partner choose the direction

Set up two short front-court destinations, one to each side. Begin with shadow movement and a partner pointing left or right. Wait for the cue, move towards the indicated area, make a controlled reach and recover. Your partner should choose the next direction only after you are ready again.

This is where a memorised pattern becomes a decision. It is tempting to alternate left, right, left, right because the exercise looks smoother. That is fine for learning the routes, but it removes the need to read the cue. When you are ready for the reaction version, let the order vary without making the pace faster.

Keep both destinations within reach of comfortable movement. You may need different racket preparation for the two sides. The front-court backhand side is not simply the forehand arm stretched across as far as it will go. Give yourself time to organise the racket while your feet put you in position. If that is too much to manage, practise each side separately again.

When the pointing version is manageable, progress to easy shuttle feeds. Have your partner send the shuttle to one of the two agreed areas. Read the feed, move, play a simple return and recover. Do not add difficult spinning net shots or full-court coverage at the same time. Change one part of the exercise and see whether the movement survives it.

When you still feel late, look at the whole sequence

You start after the shuttle is already well on its way. Watch the moments before your first push. You may be standing still until the direction is obvious. Return to the single-direction cue drill so you can work on preparation without also solving the route.

You reach the shuttle but cannot leave the lunge. Shorten the distance and check where your weight finishes. Reaching farther with the upper body can make the racket arrive while the rest of you falls behind. Practise the recoverable movement first; do not judge the repetition only by whether you touched the shuttle.

You take several tiny rescue steps at the end. The problem may have started earlier. Perhaps the first movement was hesitant, or the approach left you too close to the shuttle. Use a predictable destination and watch the approach as well as the final step. A few adjustment steps are normal; frantic correction on every attempt is a reason to simplify.

You race back and miss the next cue. Recovery should put you in a position to respond. If you are still charging towards a floor mark when your partner starts, slow down the sequence. Ask for a longer reset and finish balanced before shortening the interval.

A short recording can help you tell these apart. Keep the camera outside the playing area and include both feet, the target and the recovery. Watch one attempt at normal speed first. Then look again at the particular moment you are trying to change. A dramatic slow-motion lunge is less useful than seeing whether you were ready for the next movement.

Take one badminton footwork drill into your next session

Choose the exercise that matches the problem you can actually see. If the landing is unstable, start with the short lunge. If you can move well on your own but hesitate when someone feeds, work on the cue. If predictable movement is comfortable, try the two-direction version.

Keep attempts short enough that you can still notice and change something. Rest when the quality drops, and return to an easier version if you begin stumbling or reaching beyond your control. There is no prize for finishing a complicated pattern badly. Before you stop, repeat the original easy movement and check whether the change is still there.

For the next session, write down one useful observation: “I can push back from the shorter lunge,” or “I move better when I watch the feed instead of counting.” That gives you a place to begin next time. Speed can come later; first make the trip to the shuttle and back something you can repeat.
