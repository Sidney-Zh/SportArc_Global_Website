Table Tennis Forehand Drills: Three Ways to Build Consistency

Practice these table tennis forehand drills on one steady line, then add a target and small sideways movements.

A forehand can feel easy for two or three balls. Then one comes a little closer to your body, you reach for it, and the rally is over. If that sounds familiar, adding more power probably isn't the next thing to work on.

Start with a ball you can repeat. Give yourself time to get into position, make a comfortable stroke and recover. These table tennis forehand drills build that habit in small steps: keep one line going, aim into a generous target area, then add a little movement.

You will need a table, a paddle, a few balls and a partner who can return or feed the ball predictably. A robot can also provide a steady feed. The instructions describe a right-handed player; reverse left and right if you play left-handed. Work with comfortable, light-topspin or nearly flat balls. Opening against heavy backspin is a different task.

Before you start these table tennis forehand drills

For these drills, use a compact forehand drive. You are trying to send a comfortable ball back to your partner, with enough clearance over the net to keep the rally going. A big backswing makes that harder than it needs to be.

Begin in a balanced position with your knees comfortably bent and some space between your elbow and your body. Hold the paddle securely without squeezing it throughout the stroke. Turn a little through your torso as the ball approaches, then let your arm move forwards with that turn. Meet the ball in front of you rather than waiting until it has crowded your hip.

The paddle travels forwards and a little upwards. Its angle depends on the incoming ball and your stroke, so don't try to hold it at one exact angle for every shot. A slightly closed face is a useful starting point against a gentle topspin feed. If the ball repeatedly flies long or drops into the net, adjust one thing at a time with your partner.

After contact, let the stroke finish naturally and bring the paddle back to a comfortable ready position. You should feel able to play another ball. If every forehand leaves you twisted, stretched or falling sideways, slow the feed down before trying to hit harder.

Drill 1: Keep a steady forehand rally on one line

The aim: make the same comfortable contact several times in a row.

Start with forehand-to-forehand practice, usually across the diagonal when both players are right-handed. Agree on a gentle pace. Your partner's job is to return the ball to a manageable area, not to test your reach or try to win the point.

If neither of you can keep that feed going yet, have one person feed individual balls. Start each attempt from a balanced ready position. A short sequence of good feeds is more useful than a long rally full of last-second lunges.

  1. Send the ball towards the middle of your partner's forehand area, leaving room away from the edges.
  2. Use a short backswing and meet the ball in front of you.
  3. Recover as soon as your stroke finishes, so you can watch the next ball from a ready position.
  4. When you miss, reset rather than immediately speeding up the next attempt.

Count the comfortable returns if that helps you stay focused, but don't turn the number into the whole exercise. A rally where you are balanced and in control is more useful than a longer one you survive by reaching.

Watch for: a similar contact point from ball to ball, an easy rhythm and enough time to recover. Your partner should not have to chase every return.

Make it easier: slow down, reduce the length of each exchange or use individual feeds. If the feed itself is inconsistent, fix that first.

Move on when: you can repeat the line over several attempts without rushing your swing. You don't need a perfect rally before introducing the next drill.

Drill 2: Give your forehand a target

The aim: keep your normal stroke while choosing where the ball lands.

Use the same comfortable feed, but agree on a broad target area on the other side. Start with a generous portion of the table rather than a tiny corner. You can use the existing table lines and agree on an imaginary zone; there is no need to put loose objects on the playing surface.

Hit towards that area at the same pace you used in the first drill. Keep your attention on the ball's landing point, then notice what changed when you missed. Did you arrive late? Did your swing suddenly get bigger? Did you try to steer the ball with your wrist at the last moment?

Repeat one target long enough to get a feel for it. Then pause and choose a different target. At first, avoid changing targets randomly during the rally. You are learning to make a deliberate adjustment, not testing how many decisions you can handle at once.

A partner returning the ball may need to change position when you change your target. Agree on the new pattern before restarting. With individual feeds or a robot, keep the incoming placement predictable while you work on your outgoing direction.

Watch for: whether the ball reaches the chosen area without a sudden increase in effort. Keep a simple note of the shots that land in the zone and the usual direction of your misses. This is a practice record, not a test with a required pass mark.

Make it easier: enlarge the target or go back to your more comfortable line. If aiming makes your stroke tense, take a few easy rallies before trying again.

Move on when: changing the target no longer makes you abandon the compact stroke you built in the first drill.

Drill 3: Add a small sideways adjustment

The aim: arrive at the ball with enough space to make the same forehand.

Ask your partner to feed two nearby positions on your forehand side in an agreed order. Keep the distance small. This is not a full-table footwork drill, and you shouldn't need to sprint or stretch to reach either ball.

Begin slowly enough to separate the jobs: move into position, play the forehand, recover. Use short adjustment steps rather than reaching farther with your arm. Stay balanced and avoid letting your feet collide or cross awkwardly as you move.

At each position, look for roughly the same comfortable spacing between your body and the ball. The purpose of moving is to preserve that spacing. If one shot feels cramped and the other feels like a reach, reduce the distance between the feeds.

Once the pattern feels manageable, let the movement and preparation become more continuous. Keep the order predictable for now. Random feeds can come later; adding uncertainty and distance together makes it difficult to tell what is causing the miss.

Watch for: whether your second forehand looks and feels like your first. Notice what happens after contact, too. Can you recover without an extra stumble or a hurried step?

Make it easier: practice one position, take a short pause, then move to the other. You can also rehearse the small movement without a ball before returning to live feeds.

Move on when: you can maintain balance and comfortable contact at both positions. Add a little distance or a little unpredictability next, rather than increasing everything at once.

When the same mistake keeps coming back

A repeated miss is useful information. Resist the urge to change your grip, feet and entire swing after one bad ball. Start with the pattern you can see.

The ball keeps going long. Check whether you have started swinging harder or opening the paddle face. Return to the gentle feed and shorten the action. Make a small adjustment, then give it a few attempts before changing again.

The ball keeps hitting the net. Notice whether contact is late or low, or whether the paddle face is too closed for the incoming ball. Give yourself a little more clearance and check your starting position. Don't solve every net ball by flicking your wrist.

You get crowded by the ball. Adjust your feet before contact rather than pulling your elbow tightly against your ribs. Ask for a slower feed while you learn to recognise the spacing.

The first ball is good, but the next one falls apart. Look at your recovery. A large finish can leave you with too much work to do before the next shot. Keep the pace comfortable and return to ready earlier.

If a movement causes pain, stop that movement. These are skill practices, not a reason to push through discomfort.

Put one useful change into your next session

You don't have to complete all three drills every time you play. Start with the first one and choose the next step that matches your problem. If your rally breaks down without movement, stay with the steady feed. If you keep the ball on but can't choose a landing area, spend more time on the target drill.

Keep practice blocks short enough that you can still concentrate on the task. Pause when the quality starts to drop, talk to your partner and restart with one clear cue. There is no universal number of repetitions that makes a forehand consistent.

A short recording can help you compare the first few balls with the ones just before a miss. Look at your distance from the ball, the size of your swing and whether you recover. Choose one observation to take back to the table.

For your next session, a useful goal is simple: finish more forehands ready to play another one. Once that feels normal, you have a much better base for adding pace and spin.
